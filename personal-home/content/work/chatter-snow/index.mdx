casestudy:
  problem: >
    There was no infrastructure. A shared Gmail account and an Instagram profile were the
    entire operational stack: no domain, no organizational identity, no records, and no system
    to track donated gear, coordinate volunteers, manage events, handle finances, or organize
    donations. Everything lived in DMs, threads, and individual people's heads, with no
    visibility for community members. The core issue: Chatter couldn't tell people "here's
    what we have available." More importantly for a group organizing toward nonprofit status,
    it couldn't tell anyone, including itself, what impact it was actually having.
  context: >
    Chatter Snow is not a nonprofit yet; the group is actively organizing to become one. That
    framing drove the whole project. The infrastructure exists so the organization can measure
    and evidence its impact (gear distributed, members served, events run, money in and out),
    which is exactly what a nonprofit application and future funders will ask for. Add a
    volunteer team, effectively no budget, a growing inventory of donated ski and snowboard
    gear, and an active community that wanted to know what was available. Being both a board
    member and the technical lead meant seeing the operational need and the technical
    constraints firsthand.
  goals:
    - Stand up real infrastructure (domain, email, identity, accounts) where none existed
    - Create a centralized inventory system
    - Let the community see available gear without a DM or an email
    - Capture the operational data needed to evidence impact for nonprofit formation
    - Give the board a real record of meetings, minutes, decisions and money
    - Keep it maintainable by volunteers, not over-engineered
  constraints:
    - "Effectively no budget: a pre-nonprofit group with no funding stream"
    - Starting from zero infrastructure, not migrating an existing system
    - "Volunteer-driven: the system couldn't be complex"
    - "Real member, donor and financial data: nothing from production can be copied into tests, demos or screenshots"
    - No dedicated devops or infrastructure team
  research: >
    As a board member and director, I already understood the operational workflows, community
    expectations, budget reality and volunteer capabilities. The key insight at the start: with
    nothing to migrate, the risk wasn't legacy, it was over-building. Buy the commodity pieces
    (domain, mail, hosting), build only what's specific to Chatter, start narrow with
    inventory, and design for expansion.
  architecture: >
    Minimal, maintainable, and bought where possible. Two surfaces: a public site the
    community sees and an operations portal where the organization actually runs. Next.js and
    TypeScript power both, backed by Supabase/PostgreSQL for inventory, events, volunteers,
    governance and financials, with Zoho for organizational mail, Resend for transactional
    sending, Vercel for hosting, and Cloudflare for CDN and DNS. As the system matured it was
    generalized into Coven, a multi-tenant platform, and Chatter Snow now runs on it as tenant
    one.
  design: >
    Functional and clear, focused on usability over visual polish, because volunteers needed
    to understand the system quickly. The public site tells the community who Chatter is and
    what gear is available. The ops portal covers inventory, volunteer coordination and shift
    signup, events and registrations, governance (board meetings, minutes, decisions,
    conflict-of-interest disclosures), donations, expenses and reimbursements. Chatter's
    volunteers edit their own copy, photos and brand from Administration instead of asking me.
    The same brand tokens render Chatter's brand guide at chattersnow.org/brand, so the
    reference a volunteer or designer is handed can't go stale. Access is role-based, and the
    board decides what each role can do.
  implementation: >
    Phase zero was infrastructure that didn't exist: registering chattersnow.org, moving off
    the shared Gmail account to organizational email, and setting up DNS, hosting and
    accounts. The first build covered inventory, the community-facing gear view, and basic
    volunteer coordination. It then grew into the full ops portal: events, governance,
    donations and financial tracking, volunteer hours, and reporting. Each piece shipped when
    the board needed it, not on a roadmap, which kept scope tied to real use.
  challenges:
    - Starting from zero, with no domain, no org email, and no records to migrate or learn from
    - Requirement creep, because once people saw the system working everyone wanted new features
    - Designing for volunteers, so complex features had to stay simple to use
    - Wearing two hats, as a board member deciding what to build and the person building it
    - "Keeping Chatter's own brand, copy and data separate from a platform other organizations will run on"
  decisions:
    - title: "Buy the commodity, build the difference"
      description: "Introduced Zoho as the organization's email instead of building or self-administering anything, so the custom work stayed on what's specific to Chatter."
      tradeoffs: >
        Replacing the shared Gmail account with a real organizational mailbox took a day
        instead of a project, at the cost of a small recurring bill on a near-zero budget.
    - title: "Start with inventory"
      description: "Launched with gear inventory and the community's view of it before volunteers, events or finances."
      tradeoffs: >
        It answered the question the community asked most ("what's available?") and got the
        system into daily use quickly. The cost was running the rest of the organization on
        the old pile a while longer.
    - title: "Use Supabase, not Firebase"
      description: "Chose Supabase over Firebase for the database layer."
      tradeoffs: >
        Better suited to relational data (gear, volunteers, events, governance and financials
        are all interconnected), and Postgres row-level security later made it possible to
        serve other organizations safely. The cost was slightly more infrastructure to manage
        than Firebase.
    - title: "Public site and ops portal are separate surfaces"
      description: "Split the community-facing site from the operations portal, on separate hosts."
      tradeoffs: >
        A simple, focused experience for members and an easier boundary to secure, at the
        cost of some duplicated code.
    - title: "Vercel serverless functions, not a traditional backend"
      description: "Used Vercel serverless functions for all API logic instead of a standalone backend service."
      tradeoffs: >
        Simple scaling, low cost and minimal devops, at the cost of cold-start latency, which
        is acceptable for a volunteer organization's usage.
    - title: "Let Chatter become a tenant instead of keeping bespoke software"
      description: "Moved Chatter Snow onto Coven, the platform its system was generalized into, rather than maintaining a one-off codebase for one organization."
      tradeoffs: >
        Chatter gets features built for other organizations and a maintainer whose time is
        spread across more than one group, while its brand, content and data stay its own and
        can be exported. The cost is that Chatter no longer has software shaped only around
        it; anything it needs has to fit a product other organizations also use.
  result: >
    A group that ran on a shared Gmail account and an Instagram profile now has a domain,
    organizational email, a public site, and an operations portal covering inventory,
    volunteers, events, governance, donations and financials. The community can see available
    gear without sending a DM. The board has a record of its meetings, decisions and money, and
    for the first time the organization is generating the operational record it needs to show
    its impact as it organizes toward nonprofit status. Specific impact figures are still TBD,
    pending more usage data.
  lessonLearned:
    - "Infrastructure is the measurement. Chatter can't evidence its impact for nonprofit formation without a system that records what it does."
    - "Start narrow, design wide. Focusing on inventory first made launch possible, and the architecture still had room to grow into a platform."
    - "Get hands dirty. Being on the board meant understanding real problems firsthand, an edge an outside consultant wouldn't have."
    - "Separate the organization from the software early. Chatter's identity and records stayed its own when the system became a product, because that line was drawn before it mattered."

## See it running

- **[chattersnow.org](https://chattersnow.org)**: who Chatter is, and what gear is available to
  the community.
- **portal.chattersnow.org** holds real member and financial data, so access is invite-only
  and role-based. To see the portal, use **[the demo](https://demo.rickiecruz.com/portal)**:
  one click signs you in as an administrator of a fictional organization running the same
  software.

## Now running on Coven

Chatter's system became [Coven](/projects/coven), a multi-tenant operations platform for small
organizations, and Chatter Snow is its first tenant. That case study covers the platform side:
tenant isolation in the database, the public demo, and provisioning new organizations.

## Metrics

Technical metrics (deployment time, uptime, page load time, hosting cost) and organizational
impact metrics (time saved, member engagement, admin efficiency) are still TBD and will be added
once more usage data is available. Producing those impact numbers is a large part of why the
system exists: Chatter is organizing toward nonprofit status, and the record the portal
generates is what that case will be built on.

## What's next

Reporting aimed squarely at the nonprofit application: gear distributed, members served, events
run, money in and out. Longer term, fundraising integrations.
